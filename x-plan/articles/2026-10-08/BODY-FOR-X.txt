Four people fell out of orbit this morning, hit the Pacific off LA at 11:34am ET, and I'm betting most people found out from their feed between emails.

Not a complaint. That's the whole point.

### Splashdown before lunch

Crew-12 splashed down off the coast of Los Angeles at 11:34am ET today. NASA's Jessica Meir and Jack Hathaway, ESA's Sophie Adenot, Roscosmos' Andrey Fedyaev. (https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/10/08/nasas-spacex-crew-12-dragon-splashes-down-at-1134-a-m-edt/)

SpaceX needed four words for it. "Splashdown of Dragon confirmed!" (https://x.com/SpaceX/status/2108220101614850436)

237 days. More than 100 million miles. More than 3,792 orbits. They launched Feb 13 and came home on a Thursday morning like it was a work trip. (https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-spacex-crew-12-splashes-down-sets-briefing-to-discuss-mission/)

### Boring ride. Not a boring mission.

Don't mix up the ride with the job. Meir did four spacewalks this mission, seven in her career, third most of any woman at NASA. (https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-spacex-crew-12-splashes-down-sets-briefing-to-discuss-mission/)

She's at 440 total days in space now. Top 10 in NASA history. (https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/10/08/nasas-spacex-crew-12-dragon-splashes-down-at-1134-a-m-edt/)

Adenot did three spacewalks in 14 days, first French woman to do one, and just broke the European record for longest single spaceflight by more than 35 days. On her first flight. Credit where it's due, that's huge. (https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/epsilon/Splashdown_confirmed_for_Sophie_Adenot)

Hathaway was a rookie too. First trip up and he does 237 days. (https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-spacex-crew-12-splashes-down-sets-briefing-to-discuss-mission/)

[IMAGE: sec2.jpg - Crew-12 official portrait: Andrey Fedyaev, Jessica Meir, Jack Hathaway, Sophie Adenot. Photo: NASA]

### The boring is the flex

Look at how it went down. Dragon Freedom gets lifted onto the recovery ship Shannon and parked in what the teams literally call the "Dragon nest." Hatch opens, they help them out one by one. (https://x.com/esaspaceflight/status/2108227687471042858)

NPR says Crew-12 was the 20th time SpaceX has sent humans to orbit. Twenty. (https://www.npr.org/2026/10/08/nx-s1-5992845/nasa-spacex-crew-12-international-space-station-iss-splashdown)

Even NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman basically said it today: "Building the capability, experience, and confidence to do this repeatedly is exactly what will allow us to go farther." He's right. (https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-spacex-crew-12-splashes-down-sets-briefing-to-discuss-mission/)

People say space got dull. I say it's the first time it actually works like travel.

### Next truck is already loading

The station doesn't even get a weekend. SpaceX's 35th cargo run is targeted for 6:33am ET Tuesday, Oct 13, with more than 6,300 pounds aboard, including the final set of the station's roll-out solar arrays. (https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-sets-coverage-for-spacex-35th-station-resupply-launch-arrival/)

Crew-12 does their science press conference in Houston on Oct 15. (https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasas-spacex-crew-12-splashes-down-sets-briefing-to-discuss-mission/)

Thrilling is for test flights. The Moon and Mars need boring. Lots of it.

So which is it. Did space get boring, or did we finally get good at it?
